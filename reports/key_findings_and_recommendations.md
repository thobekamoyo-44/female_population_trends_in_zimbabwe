## Key Findings and Recommendations

# Zimbabwe Women's Health Analysis

1. Population and Fertility Patterns
- Zimbabwe's female population increased substantially over the period analysed. The average female population rose from approximately 2.23 million in the 1960s to 8.50 million in the 2020s.
- The average total fertility rate declined from 4.25 births per woman in the 1990s to 3.94 in the 2000s, before increasing to 4.24 in the 2010s.
- Fertility rates differed considerably across wealth groups. Women in the poorest wealth quintile (Q1) had an average fertility rate of 5.49 births per woman, compared with 2.60 among women in the richest quintile (Q5).
Key insight: Fertility rates were considerably higher among women in poorer wealth groups.

2. Contraceptive Use
- Average contraceptive use among married women increased from 50.20% in the 1990s to 62.33% in the 2000s.
- Usage increased further to 63.87% in the 2010s.
- Contraceptive use also varied by wealth. The poorest quintile recorded an average of 53.17%, while the richest quintile recorded 68.34%.
- A strong negative correlation of approximately -0.97 was observed between contraceptive use and fertility across the five wealth quintiles.
Key insight: Wealthier groups generally reported higher contraceptive use and lower fertility rates. However, this relationship does not establish that contraceptive use alone explains differences in fertility.

3. Access to Healthcare
- The average percentage of women reporting difficulty accessing healthcare because of distance to a health facility increased from 33.36% in the 1990s to 44.86% in the 2000s.
- The percentage subsequently declined to 36.86% in the 2010s.
- Differences across wealth groups were substantial. The poorest quintile recorded 58.63%, compared with 9.78% among the richest quintile.
Key insight: Women in poorer wealth groups experienced considerably greater difficulties accessing healthcare facilities due to distance.

The increase during the 2000s coincided with Zimbabwe's economic crisis, while the subsequent decline coincided with a period of economic stabilisation. These historical developments may provide context, but the analysis does not establish a direct causal relationship.

4. Age at First Marriage
- The average median age at first marriage increased from 19.07 years in the 1990s to 19.68 years in the 2010s.
- Differences were also observed across wealth groups.
- Women in the poorest quintile had a median age at first marriage of 18.56 years, compared with 20.74 years among women in the richest quintile.
Key insight: Women in wealthier groups generally married later than women in poorer groups.

5. Teenage Pregnancy and Motherhood
- Teenage pregnancy and motherhood increased from an average of 20.70% in the 1990s to 23.86% in the 2010s.
- Wealth differences were particularly noticeable.
- The poorest quintile recorded an average of 31.52%, while the richest quintile recorded 9.34%.
Key insight: Teenage pregnancy and motherhood were substantially more common among women in poorer wealth groups.

## Recommendations

Based on the findings, the following areas deserve attention:

1. Improve healthcare accessibility: Prioritise healthcare facilities and services in communities where women experience greater difficulty accessing healthcare.

2. Strengthen family planning services: Improve access to affordable contraception and reproductive health information, particularly among lower-income communities.

3. Support teenage reproductive health: Strengthen reproductive health education and support services for teenage girls, especially in disadvantaged communities.

4. Address socioeconomic inequalities: Consider how differences in household wealth relate to reproductive health outcomes and access to services.

5. Continue monitoring reproductive health indicators: Regular monitoring can help identify changes in fertility, teenage pregnancy and healthcare accessibility.

## Limitations of the Analysis
- The analysis focuses on selected women's health indicators available in the dataset.
- The dataset does not contain direct education indicators, limiting the ability to investigate the relationship between education and reproductive health.

- Some indicators have observations for different periods, so not all comparisons cover the same years.
- The analysis identifies associations and patterns but does not establish cause-and-effect relationships.
- Averages across decades and wealth groups may hide differences within individual years and populations.

## Conclusion
The analysis reveals notable differences in women's reproductive health outcomes across wealth groups in Zimbabwe. Women in poorer wealth quintiles generally experienced higher fertility rates, higher teenage pregnancy and motherhood rates, lower contraceptive use, and greater difficulty accessing healthcare facilities.

The findings demonstrate the importance of considering socioeconomic inequalities when examining fertility, contraceptive use, teenage pregnancy and access to healthcare.
